## 2. Training (scorer fitted on the validation split, then frozen)

"Training" here means fitting the reliability scorer, not the LLM:

* **Data:** 3,600 (memory, val-task) pairs. They come from 60 val tasks × 3 val seeds (100–102) × 5 corruption rates × top-4 retrieved memories, giving 4,930 rows once verification states are included. 56.1% of rows are reliable.
* **Signal weights:** L2 logistic regression. Grouped 5-fold CV ROC-AUC is **0.838** (folds 0.826–0.858). The fitted weights are: version match +2.12, confidence +1.40, verification pass +0.94, verification fail −4.75, outcome −0.97, recency +0.23, context similarity +0.11, bias −0.89. The contradiction signal is a hard gate and is not weighted.
* **Confidence calibration:** Platt scaling on 1,784 val memories. Brier score drops from 0.646 with the raw self-reported confidence to **0.228** (isotonic: 0.234). The Platt slope is negative. Corrupted memories keep the confident self-reports of the episodes they were made from, so on this data *high self-reported confidence predicts lower reliability*.
* **Thresholds:** a grid search maximises val utility (+1 for using a reliable memory, −2 for an unreliable one, −0.05 per probe), subject to wrongly rejecting ≤10% of safe memories. The top 3 settings were then re-checked with real LLM runs on val (30% corruption). The setting `use ≥ 0.65, reject < 0.60` gave the best val success (81.7% vs 78.3% for the simulation-optimal `use ≥ 0.90`) with fewer tool calls, and was frozen.
* **Freezing:** `artifacts/FROZEN.json` stores sha256 hashes of all 9 scorers (trained, hand-weighted, 6 signal ablations, no-hard-gate). `run_experiments.py` refuses to run on the test split if any hash changes.

**Reading the results:**
* **Memory helps a lot.** All memory conditions beat no-memory (63.9%) by about 13–24 points.
* **Fixed bank.** Ours (trained) has the highest success at every corruption rate (81.7–86.1%). Pooled over rates, it beats plain retrieval by **+6.4 pp [1.8, 11.2]**, outcome-only by **+5.7 pp [1.1, 10.3]** and Xiong-style by **+5.4 pp [0.8, 10.0]**. It is *not* significantly better than Reflexion-style (+1.9 pp [−3.1, 7.2]) or ours-hand (+3.4 pp [−0.2, 7.2]).
* **Growing bank.** Ours (trained) is best or tied-best at most rates. The only significant gain at a single rate is over Xiong-style at 30% (+8.3 pp). Pooled over rates, gains of +2.3 to +4.7 pp over the other memory conditions are **not significant**, and it is tied with Reflexion-style (−0.3 pp).
* **Corruption barely lowers task success.** Plain retrieval stays at 76.7–77.2% across 0–50% corruption in the fixed bank. The agent often recovers from bad memories by itself, using `help`, lookups and error messages. The damage from corruption shows up in *error* metrics (sections 4–5) far more than in success.

## 9. Answers to the research questions (LR §6.1)

**Main question:** can reliability-oriented episodic memory reduce the transmission of errors from unreliable experiences?
**Yes, for error transmission; only partly for task success.**
* Against plain retrieval at 50% corruption in the growing bank, the trained policy cut erroneous descendant memories by about 4× (2.67 vs 10.0 per run) and halved paired-attributed errors (6.7% vs 13.3% of tasks).
* It cut repeated errors by 4–7× in both modes.
* The task-success gains are significant in the fixed bank (+5 to +6 pp over plain, outcome-only and Xiong-style) but not in the growing bank.

**RQ1:** How does the proportion of unreliable memories affect errors and task efficiency?
* For baselines, the reliable fraction of *used* memories falls roughly linearly with corruption (plain: 0.47 at 0%, 0.23 at 50%, fixed bank).
* Erroneous descendants grow with the corruption rate (plain: 2.7 at 10% to 10.0 at 50%; Reflexion-style: 6.0 to 20.0).
* Paired-attributed errors rise from 3.9% to 11.1% (plain, fixed).
* Task success itself is surprisingly robust (plain is flat at about 77% in the fixed bank), because the agent often self-corrects with tools. Efficiency (tokens and tool calls) barely changes with the corruption rate.

**RQ2:** Which reliability signals add value over outcome-only screening?
* Outcome-only screening alone performs like plain retrieval: success is never significantly different, the reliable-used fraction is the same, and the repeated-error rate is 15–19%.
* In the ablations, the **hard contradiction gate**, **outcome** (inside the combined score) and **volatility-aware recency** are the signals whose removal significantly hurts at 50% corruption.
* **Version match** carries the largest learned weight.
* Confidence, context similarity and verification show no measurable effect on success at the frozen thresholds. Confidence is informative only after calibration, and with a *negative* slope.

**RQ3:** Does the verify/correct/reject policy reduce error chains compared with other memory types?
* **Yes against plain, outcome-only and Reflexion-style memory.** At 50% corruption, erroneous descendants are 2.67 per run for ours (trained), against 10.0, 9.33 and 20.0. Max depth is 1.33 against 2.0, 1.33 and 2.0.
* **Comparable to Xiong-style:** 2.33 descendants per run. Xiong-style blocks propagation by filtering what gets *written*; ours does it by filtering what gets *reused*. Ours, though, has higher success and fewer attributed errors (6.7% vs 13.9% at 50%, growing bank).
* Reflexion-style lesson memory propagates the most. Its lessons detach conventions from their evidence, and it has the highest repeated-error rate (21–34%).

**RQ4:** How do reliability, memory volume, efficiency and cost trade off?
* Ours injects about half as many memories (1.5–2.1 vs 4.0 per task), uses 25–31% fewer prompt tokens than plain retrieval, and makes about the same number of LLM and tool calls.
* The price is **unnecessary rejection of 9–14% of safe memories**, above the 10% cap targeted on val at higher corruption rates, and the absence of verification at the chosen thresholds.
* The hand-weighted variant shows the other side of the trade-off: it never rejects safe memories (0%), but uses many more unreliable ones (reliable-used 0.35–0.78) and is less accurate.

## 11. Honest limitations

1. **Synthetic world.** The environment, tasks and corruption types were designed by us. The effect sizes say how the mechanism behaves *in this world*, not on real tools or web agents.
2. **One backbone** (gpt-oss-20b), one retriever (MiniLM), k=4. The Groq cross-check is incomplete.
3. **60 test tasks × 3 seeds.** CIs are about ±6–7 pp per cell, so many cell-level differences are not significant. The secondary pooled analysis is reported separately.
4. **Verification was effectively disabled on test** by the LLM-confirmed thresholds. The effect of the verify/revise branch on test success is therefore not measured. The revise logic is implemented and unit-tested, and fires in growing mode when it is reached.
5. **The verification oracle is strong when it applies.** Tool probes return the true current value. We limited this deliberately: rules such as date order and units have no probe and stay "unchecked".
6. **The ground-truth definition is ours.** An honest failure record counts as "reliable". This drives the negative outcome weight, because corrupted memories almost always carry success labels. Lesson-text claims are extracted only for unambiguous tokens.
7. **Unnecessary rejection (9–14%) exceeded the 10% val cap** at higher corruption rates on test.
8. **Latency** is measured under shared, concurrent vLLM load and is not a deployment latency.
9. **vLLM batching is not bit-deterministic.** The response cache guarantees reproducibility of these results, but a fresh live rerun may differ slightly.
